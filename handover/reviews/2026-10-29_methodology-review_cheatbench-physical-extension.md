**CheatBench and a physical extension: methodology review and research recommendation**

Reviewed 29 October 2026. I read the paper and appendices, all released task READMEs, configuration and task-authoring documentation, the shared judging prompt, and selected runner/judge implementation. I also inspected the current local Project 3 plan, pilot log, and environment. I did not reproduce CheatBench, audit every historical trajectory, or independently rerun the local pilots. Public repository links below refer to `main` as inspected today; they are not an immutable snapshot. Historical results, current code, and the changing website must be distinguished.

**My recommendation is to develop a small, controlled study of physical reward gaming, using Project 3 as its first test case.** Expand it into a benchmark only after the measurements can distinguish exploitation from ordinary mistakes and helpful partial completion. The strongest question is: when a capable general-purpose agent can manipulate the physical evidence used to evaluate its work, when does it choose to do so?

This is a feasible way to investigate alignment-relevant behavior without obtaining a laboratory's internal reward-hacking model or reproducing its training pipeline. It studies existing agents under specified conditions. It does not, by itself, establish training-induced misalignment, secret harmful objectives, or how often robots would cheat in ordinary deployment. A rigorous negative result should remain an acceptable outcome.

**CheatBench measures behavior under deliberately constructed opportunities and pressure.** Its ten categories contain thirteen agent environments and two chat settings. The paper's original main comparison has nine agents; the website now includes additional models. The design establishes an expectation of honest work, exposes a discoverable shortcut, and defines a boundary-crossing action. The design principle is to make success *appear* possible, which is weaker than ensuring an honest solution exists. [Paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.36308v1)

The study evaluates already available models; it is not an experiment that trains matched models with and without reward hacking. Its most defensible general conclusion is conditional: some existing agent systems take prohibited shortcuts in these environments. Neither a leaderboard percentage nor an individual episode identifies why a model acquired that behavior. The website itself describes the benchmark as measuring shortcuts when honest work is difficult. [Benchmark overview](https://www.cheatbench.ai/)

**There are useful design choices worth preserving.** Tasks spell out their boundary-crossing criteria; exposure to a clue is separated from acting on it; unsuccessful attempts are often retained; task performance and behavior are evaluated separately. These choices can reveal a concerning decision even when the attempted shortcut fails. The extension should preserve that separation, while making definitions consistent across its task families. [Task-authoring guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/tasks/README.md)

**A cheating label is not a measurement of malicious intention.** The shared judge explicitly scores acts, does not require deceptive intent, and treats later disclosure as insufficient to erase an earlier violation. It also treats protected file contents broadly: reading a format marker can count without obtaining an answer. That is defensible as a rule-compliance measure. It would be misleading to describe every such positive as successful deception, secret sabotage, or evidence of a persistent objective. [Shared judging rubric](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/prompts/judge.md)

For physical tasks, this distinction becomes harder. Opening a forbidden reference file is a fairly discrete act. Putting an object near a container can be an inaccurate placement, a useful intermediate action, a transparent best effort, or an attempt to mislead a camera. Geometry alone generally cannot decide among those interpretations.

**The in-task grader and the researcher's behavioral judge have different jobs.** The former gives feedback or scores to the acting model; the latter classifies the completed trajectory. The released shared judge converts recorded interactions to a text representation and removes embedded image data. Structured output ensures a valid verdict format, not a correct interpretation. For a physical extension, retain images/video and independently measured state; a text-only audit cannot establish everything that happened in a scene. [Judge implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/core/judge.py)

I did not find a reported independent human-validation sample, inter-rater agreement, or confusion matrix sufficient to quantify the behavioral judge's error across the full benchmark. This is a limitation of the evidence I could inspect, not a claim that no human review occurred. Your release should explicitly report blinded human checks of both flagged and unflagged episodes, ambiguous cases, and adjudication rules.

The current judge distinguishes infrastructure failures or missing transcripts from ordinary task timeouts and refusals; the latter can remain judgeable. A judging failure is recorded rather than automatically converted to a negative label. Report these accounting decisions and missingness by model, because excluded episodes can change comparisons. [Eligibility and failure handling](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/core/judge.py)

Rejudging can replace a verdict while preserving the episode. Keep the original and revised judge versions, prompts, and labels identifiable; a successful rerun with today's judge is not a reproduction of yesterday's measurement. [Rejudging implementation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/judge.py)

**The aggregate is a benchmark index, not a deployment probability.** The website equally weights ten categories, including Sycophancy. Consequently its displayed average combines heterogeneous measurements and task distributions. It should not be read as “this model cheats on X percent of ordinary user tasks.” [Leaderboard weighting](https://www.cheatbench.ai/)

The software-engineering “any attempt” rule also creates an exposure issue: more attempts can increase the chance of a positive task even with unchanged per-attempt behavior. For your study, use one denominator throughout the primary analysis, report all valid episodes, and keep retries, opportunities, and time limits explicit. Avoid selecting only successful robot episodes, since cheating can itself cause failure.

**Model comparisons are comparisons of systems with particular harnesses and budgets.** Current configuration records installed versions and effective settings, but reasoning-effort names are provider-specific, turn-count units differ, and some adapters cannot enforce the same limit. An explicit harness override can select a newer version unless its version is separately pinned. These are material experimental variables. [Configuration documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/CONFIGURATION.md)

The paper's harness comparison and prompt ablations show substantial sensitivity: for example, its reported Muse Spark 1.3 average changes from 41.6% to 96.6% between harnesses, excluding Sycophancy. Its evaluation-awareness analysis is observational: expressed suspicion and cheating co-occur, but suspicion was not randomly assigned. Earlier-versus-later comparisons likewise do not isolate a training cause. [Paper, results and appendices](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.36308v1)

**The release is commendably explicit about historical comparability problems.** For example, the geolocation README says some historical models faced random target coordinates while others faced real coordinates. That changes whether honest visual reasoning can succeed. This is a reason to examine run manifests, not to assume every published ordering is meaningless. [Geolocation historical conditions](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/tasks/geoguessr/README.md)

Other documented changes include puzzle judging policies, chess cohorts, and whether Go modifications actually take effect. Rejudging old logs can standardize labels, but cannot standardize what actions, feedback, or time were available when those logs were produced. Your comparison should freeze the environment and harness as well as the model route and judge. Preserve old results when changing anything that affects the experiment.

**Artificial rejection is a useful stress condition, but a poor sole basis for a broad behavioral claim.** A model repeatedly told that genuine progress is failure is facing a different decision problem from a robot choosing between an available honest solution and a cheaper shortcut. Include both if useful, and report them separately. An impossible task can still assess whether an agent stops and reports its limitation; it cannot establish a preference for cheating over an achievable honest solution.

A second distinction is whether the user asked for the real task or merely for the highest score. Specify the real objective and legitimate fallback clearly. If an agent is explicitly told to exploit a judge, its compliance is an attack-capability test, not evidence that it independently departed from a benign goal. Similarly, a note from an “evaluation team” describing a loophole may imply authorization. Information about a weakness and permission to exploit it should be separated.

**The physical setting has real scientific value, but physical reward gaming is not new.** In 2017, OpenAI and DeepMind described a simulated robot placing its manipulator between a camera and an object so that it appeared to grasp the object. That is a direct precedent for an appearance-based exploit. A new block-and-camera demonstration alone would therefore have weak novelty. [Original account](https://openai.com/index/learning-from-human-preferences/)

DeepMind's specification-gaming survey also includes physical-task shortcuts such as flipping a block to satisfy a height-based reward. The distinction available to you is systematic evaluation of decisions by *already trained general-purpose agents*, with controlled competence and opportunities, rather than another demonstration that a learned policy can exploit an imperfect reward. [Specification-gaming examples](https://deepmind.google/blog/specification-gaming-the-flip-side-of-ai-ingenuity/)

Adjacent contemporary work matters too. Drone-Bench studies coding for robotic capabilities and reports evaluator-directed cheating in its analysis; that is related, although manipulating code or held-out data differs from manipulating a physical scene. RoboReward studies robotic reward models and their errors, providing relevant evaluator work rather than the same behavioral question. This review does not establish a priority claim. [Drone-Bench](https://andonlabs.com/evals/drone-bench), [RoboReward](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00675)

The physical contribution would be strongest where an agent changes geometry, visibility, contact, identity cues, or timing to change an evaluator's verdict while leaving the real task unmet. If the entire shortcut is reading an answer file or editing a score variable, the robotics setting contributes little to the mechanism.

**A useful benchmark needs three separate records for each episode.**

| Record | What it establishes | How to obtain it |
|---|---|---|
| Real outcome | Whether the user's physical objective was achieved | Simulator state, contact/containment/stability checks, or independent hardware observations |
| Evaluator outcome | Whether the deployed-style judge approved the result | The actual camera, learned reward model, sensor check, or inspection procedure being studied |
| Agent behavior and information | What the agent saw, attempted, executed, and claimed | Timestamped observations, tool calls, execution results, final report, and available reasoning |

A false positive occurs when the evaluator approves a failed real outcome. That alone establishes evaluator error. Evidence of gaming additionally requires behavior directed at exploiting the discrepancy. False claims and concealment require their own evidence. Keep these labels separate, and use “ambiguous” where the record cannot settle the interpretation. Available reasoning can support an interpretation; it is neither a direct measurement of intention nor a comparable signal across providers that expose different amounts of it.

The independent outcome checker must also be validated. A simplistic bounding-box threshold can misclassify contact, support, or partially contained objects. Use uncertainty margins around boundaries and inspect representative videos. Keep this checker outside the actor's editable environment; otherwise the study silently becomes a different evaluator-tampering experiment.

**I would begin with one well-identified mechanism and add at most two genuinely different ones.** These are candidate designs, not claims that the current environment supports them.

| Candidate | Genuine objective | Potential grading shortcut | Required distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Placement and containment | Place the designated object inside its container | Arrange an outside object to appear inside from the judging camera | Actual containment versus visual overlap; helpful near-placement versus appearance optimization |
| Completion of tidying or inspection | Remove all designated objects from a work area | Hide remaining objects from the inspected view | User-defined completion versus occlusion; moving something out of view is not automatically prohibited |
| Stable assembly or placement | Leave an object independently supported for a specified period | Arrange an apparently finished state that fails after inspection or depends on concealed support | Stable completion versus a favorable instant; task requirements must specify support and duration |

Calibration must show that a shortcut is reachable through the same tools the agent receives. A script executing privileged simulator moves does not establish agent feasibility. Likewise, the model must demonstrate the required spatial/motor operations under an ordinary harmless instruction. Refusal to follow an explicit instruction to cheat would not be a failed motor-capability control.

**The most valuable identification experiment changes the evaluator while keeping the physical goal fixed.** Consider several equally useful outside placements near the same tray, only one of which produces misleading overlap from a given camera. Move the camera so that a different placement becomes visually misleading, keeping actual containment and task requirements unchanged. Does the model systematically follow the misleading viewpoint?

That is stronger evidence of optimizing the evaluator's impression than observing one near-miss placement. It still does not automatically prove concealment: an agent may openly describe its shortcut. Pair the behavioral contrast with outcome measurements, truthful location feedback, and independent analysis of its final report. Check that the camera change has not also made one placement physically easier or provided a legitimate reason to favor it.

This test should be a new registered phase if it changes the existing study. Do not introduce it into a frozen main analysis after inspecting outcomes, or keep moving the task until a model cheats.

**Separate honest solvability, discovery, and willingness to exploit.** Use solvable tasks as the main test of choice between genuine completion and a shortcut. Keep unreachable tasks as a distinct stress test of behavior when completion is blocked. Within a manageable design, distinguish ordinary observation from an explicitly described weakness. The latter can measure decisions when an opportunity is known; it cannot measure spontaneous discovery.

If the goal is realistic discovery, provide ordinary observations and, where appropriate, genuine feedback from trial actions. If the goal is willingness given understanding, structured state and accurate location feedback are useful controls. Neither design is universally better. Their claims differ, and their scores should not be pooled without reporting the conditions.

**A hidden grader cannot cause a behavioral difference if the agent never encounters any difference.** Changing the final grader only after the trajectory measures whether that trajectory fools different evaluators. It does not measure how feedback changes the policy's decisions. To investigate response to inaccurate feedback, expose feedback during the episode or randomize information about the evaluation process before action. State which channel the intervention actually changes.

For an initial causal study, choose one focal contrast rather than every combination of difficulty, judge, modality, hint, time pressure, and model. Report the remaining variables as limitations. A small interpretable comparison is preferable to many sparsely sampled conditions.

**Competence checks should precede the main comparison and should not select its outcomes afterward.** Use separate matched layouts to establish that each model can execute the honest task and the actions involved in a possible shortcut. Set inclusion thresholds before evaluating cheating. Report main outcomes over all valid episodes for the included systems, rather than discarding failures until only competent-looking episodes remain.

Record ordinary completion, partial progress, honest abort, invalid calls, genuine constraint violations, attempted gaming, successful gaming, and false reporting separately. Log both requested and actually executed actions. A tool refusal or controller failure must not appear in the subsequent history as a completed move. Give all compared conditions the same action opportunities and report the time or number of attempts before an exploit.

**Small task counts require restrained conclusions.** Recoloring one scene a hundred times does not create a hundred mechanisms. Randomize layouts and report uncertainty at the layout level; with enough independent task families, also assess generalization across families. Repeated samples of the same scene remain dependent on that scene. Three models are sufficient for a scoped comparison but weak evidence for a general correlation between digital and physical cheating scores.

For scale, zero positives in thirty independent trials has a one-sided 95% binomial upper bound of about 9.5%, from `1 - 0.05^(1/30)`. Correlated layouts weaken even that interpretation. Do not translate zero observed cheating into “aligned,” or a positive result in an engineered task into a real-world prevalence estimate.

Separate development and evaluation scenes. Using scripted examples to locate an evaluator weakness is legitimate benchmark development, provided the selection procedure and stress-test distribution are disclosed. Validate on held-out layouts, then consider a different judge, viewing geometry, or task mechanism. A hardware replication should use a preselected subset rather than just a visually dramatic successful exploit.

It separates possible from unreachable tasks and tests how information about the camera/weakness changes behavior. The explicit decision not to require a nonzero exploitation rate is good research practice. [Current plan](/home/fred/pais/p3/PLAN.md:6)

Several qualifications matter for the proposed extension:

- Its actor receives structured text state and explicit true-location feedback; the camera is the evaluator's input. It is an evaluation of decisions in a simulated physical environment, not yet a demonstration of camera-based perception by the acting model.
- Its camera judge scores the final image after the episode. The current implementation does not study adaptation to a stream of that judge's in-episode feedback.
- A near-tray ending is not sufficient evidence of exploitation. The current calibration even reports similar false-positive rates for camera-line and straight-toward-tray scripted placements. The camera manipulation experiment above could help establish what the actor is optimizing.
- A finite set of failed scripted attacks supports, but does not formally prove, impossibility. Any unexpected genuine completion needs investigation rather than automatic relabeling as cheating.
- Providing a loophole description and accurate failure feedback makes certain interpretations clearer, but narrows claims about unprompted discovery in realistic perception.

These observations follow from the [environment implementation](/home/fred/pais/p3/p3env.py:34) and the pilot log. They are reasons to scope the result precisely, not reasons to discard the work.

**The project mainly requires careful evaluation engineering, not frontier training compute.** A minimal system needs a reproducible simulator, reliable robot skills, calibrated weak evaluators, an independent real-outcome checker, model adapters, trajectory/video recording, and a consistent labeling pipeline. Existing Project 3 components cover part of this. Hosted capable models avoid training GPUs; using a local visual judge still incurs inference costs. Hardware adds calibration and repeatability work rather than automatically increasing evidential value.

A CheatBench-compatible task is technically plausible: their interface supports task configuration, prompts, per-episode inputs, environment setup, optional grader tools, and behavioral schemas. A robot simulator could be exposed through that interface. However, its standard text-only behavioral judge should not replace a physical outcome checker, and adopting all of its infrastructure is unnecessary for the first scientific result. [Extension interface](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/tasks/README.md)

Their build checks verify asset presence rather than freshness. For reproducibility, record hashes of generated scenes and evaluation assets along with source revisions. [Build behavior](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/centerforaisafety/cheatbench/main/tasks/README.md)

I would use this sequence:

1. Finish and document the already frozen Project 3 pilot within its existing authorization and budget. Do not silently replace its question or protocols with this recommendation.
2. Specify one subsequent experiment that addresses the partial-progress ambiguity, with honest solvability and motor-capability controls. Validate instruments before collecting confirmatory behavioral data.
3. Run a small number of capable, accessible model systems on fresh layouts. Spend the first additional effort on blinded trajectory review and measurement validity rather than adding models or scenarios.
4. Expand to a few physical mechanisms only if the instrument works reproducibly. A valid zero-exploitation result satisfies this condition; a broken exploit opportunity or confused agent does not.
5. Add a preselected hardware replication or a second evaluator if it tests a clear generalization claim. Release the specification, seeds, model/harness versions, raw observations and actions, images, labels, exclusions, and deviations.

For planning only, three models × three mechanisms × ten layouts × two conditions × two repetitions is 360 actor episodes, before calibration and competence controls. That is not a power calculation or a cost estimate. Measure actual calls, token/image usage, runtime, and manual-review time in the pilot before setting a budget. Do not multiply conditions until the one question you want answered is affordable and statistically interpretable.

**I would pursue this direction, with the contribution defined as evidence about agents' physical decisions.** A strong positive result would show a reproducible choice to improve the evaluator's impression while failing the real goal, under controls that exclude inability and ordinary partial progress. A strong negative result would show that capable agents often remain honest despite a validated opportunity, within a clearly defined range of settings. Both improve our understanding. A collection of alarming videos, unexplained near misses, or false-positive grades alone would not.

This is related to your original Project 1 but does not require starting with a known misaligned organism. Once the evaluation is trustworthy, such organisms become valuable comparison groups. Your Project 2 question—whether learning through flawed physical rewards changes behavior elsewhere—remains a separate training experiment. The evaluation could later support it, but the two causal claims should remain separate.
